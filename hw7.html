5. Discussion

Independence: theory

Two events are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A): knowing B does not change the probability of A. Two discrete random variables X and F are independent if P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x)·P(F = f) for every pair (x, f), equivalently if the conditional distribution of F is the same for every value of X, P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f). On a contingency table this means that all row profiles (conditional frequencies) coincide with the marginal profile, and likewise for the columns. With n observations, row totals ri and column totals cj, independence predicts the expected counts Eij = ricj/n, which come from the product of the marginal relative frequencies.

The chi-square test

H0: X and F are independent. The statistic χ² = Σij (Oij − Eij)²/Eij measures the distance between observed and expected counts. Under H0, for large expected counts, it is approximately χ² distributed with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom (the number of cells minus the parameters estimated from the margins). The p-value P(χ²df ≥ χ²obs) is the probability, if H0 were true, of a table at least as far from independence as the observed one (we compute it from the regularized incomplete gamma function; the permutation test, which shuffles the F labels and does not rely on the χ² approximation, is a check). A small p-value (below α) is evidence against independence; a large one only means that the data do not contradict it: not rejecting H0 is not a proof of independence, especially for small n.

How to read the results

  • Independent data (generator with s = 0): χ² is close to its degrees of freedom (the mean of a χ²df) and the p-value is typically large; but with α = 0.05 about 1 in 20 independent datasets is rejected by chance. Try several generations.
  • Dependent data (s > 0): χ² grows roughly in proportion to n (for a fixed association), so the p-value drops very quickly with the sample size, and even a weak association becomes "significant" for large n. That is why the test must be accompanied by an effect size: Cramér's V = √(χ²/(n·min(r−1, c−1))) ranges from 0 (independence) to 1 (perfect association) and does not grow with n.
  • Which cells matter. The Pearson residuals (O − E)/√E, shown in colour, tell where the dependence comes from: orange cells occur more often than independence predicts, purple ones less often. In the hair/eye data, blond hair with blue eyes and dark hair with brown eyes occur far more often than expected.
  • Validity. The usual rule is that every expected count should be at least 5 (or at most 20% of cells below 5 and none below 1). For small samples such as the "small sample" example the χ² approximation is unreliable and the permutation p-value (or Fisher's exact test for 2 × 2 tables) should be preferred; categories can also be merged.

Association is not causation

Rejecting independence shows that X and F are statistically associated: the value of one carries information about the other. It does not say why. The association may arise because X influences F, because F influences X (reverse causation), because a third variable influences both (confounding: hair and eye colour are associated mainly through common genetic ancestry, not because one colour causes the other), or by selection effects in how the sample was collected. Aggregating data can even reverse an association (Simpson's paradox). Causal claims need more than a contingency table: randomized experiments, or careful control of confounders with a causal model. Conversely, independence has a clear meaning in probability but a failure to reject it in a sample, particularly a small one, does not exclude a real dependence. Finally, statistical independence is stronger than zero correlation: independent variables are uncorrelated, but uncorrelated ones can still be dependent, and the χ² test detects any form of dependence between categorical variables, not just a linear one.
