Model and interpretation


Each attacker meets n systems one after the other and the i-th outcome Xi is i.i.d. In the Bernoulli model Xi = 1 means "system i is penetrated" (probability p), so Sn is the number of systems an attacker breaks, Sn/n the fraction of breached systems and μ = p, σ² = p(1−p). In the Rademacher model Xi = ±1 is a symmetric net outcome (+1 attacker gains ground, −1 defender wins); Sn is a zero-drift random walk with μ = 0 and σ² = 1. (Rademacher is 2·Bernoulli(1/2) − 1, so it is a Bernoulli case after centring and rescaling.) The m attackers are independent copies of the walk: they do not change the law of any single attacker's score, they give a sample of size m from which we estimate that law.


Law of Large Numbers


Sn/n has mean μ and variance σ²/n. By Chebyshev, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0, which is convergence in probability (weak LLN). Section 4 compares the empirical frequency of this event with the Chebyshev bound and with the much sharper Hoeffding bound (2e−nε²/2 for Rademacher, 2e−2nε² for Bernoulli): the true probability falls exponentially fast, and Chebyshev is only a loose guarantee. The trajectories in the middle plot show the almost-sure version (strong LLN): each individual path settles at μ. For the system owner, the fraction of breached systems becomes predictable as n grows, even though the outcome on each single system is random.


Variance under the three normalizations


Var(Sn) = nσ² grows linearly (the spread of the total grows like √n, as the envelope ±2σ√k shows); Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n shrinks to 0, so the distribution degenerates to a point mass at μ; Var(Sn/√n) = σ² stays constant. √n is the only scaling that gives a non-degenerate limit: dividing by n is too much (everything collapses), not dividing is too little (everything spreads out). The columns of section 3 show exactly this: widening, collapsing, constant width. Note that in the Bernoulli case Sn/√n has mean p√n → ∞ and therefore drifts away; the centred version (Sn − np)/√n is the one that stabilises, which is why it is shown as a fourth column.


Central Limit Theorem


In the Rademacher case Sn/√n converges in distribution to N(0,1); in the Bernoulli case (Sn − np)/√n converges to N(0, p(1−p)). The histograms approach the orange Gaussian curve as n grows. For small n the distribution is clearly discrete (lattice: Sn only takes n+1 values, only every second integer for Rademacher), and for n = 10 the fit is visibly poor, in particular when p is far from 1/2 because of the skewness. The Kolmogorov distance decreases roughly like 1/√n (Berry–Esseen). Two warnings when reading the KS column: with only m samples the statistic has a sampling noise of about 1/√m (the 5% critical value for a continuous law is ≈ 1.36/√m), and because Sn is discrete the statistic cannot go to 0 faster than about half of the largest point mass (≈ 0.4/√n for Rademacher). So it flattens at a noise floor instead of reaching 0; this does not contradict the CLT.


Effect of n (system size) and m (number of attackers)


    

  • n changes the law of the aggregate score: the expected score nμ grows linearly, the absolute fluctuation grows like σ√n, but the relative fluctuation (fluctuation of Sn/n) shrinks like σ/√n. Larger systems therefore have a larger but more predictable (relative) aggregate exposure.
    • 

  • m does not change the distribution of Sn; it only improves our picture of it (histogram noise ∝ 1/√m). Pooled over all attackers, the total score is a sum of m·n i.i.d. terms with mean mnμ and variance mnσ², and the estimate of μ from all m·n probes has standard error σ/√(mn): more attackers or more systems play the same role for this pooled estimate.
    • 

  • Caveat. The model assumes independent and identically distributed outcomes. In real systems vulnerabilities are correlated (same software, same credentials, lateral movement) and attackers adapt, which breaks independence: the variance of Sn can then grow faster than n and the Gaussian approximation can fail.
