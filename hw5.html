HW5 · Welford's recurrence and sampling statistics

Ex. 1 · Welford's recurrence formulas

Notation: x1, x2, … are the data, Mk the mean of the first k values, SSk = Σi≤k(xi − Mk)² the sum of squares about the mean, and δk = xk − Mk−1 the "surprise" of the new value.

Knuth (mean)
  M_k = (1/k) Σ_{i≤k} x_i = ((k−1)·M_{k−1} + x_k)/k = M_{k−1} + (x_k − M_{k−1})/k = M_{k−1} + δ_k/k

Two consequences, used below
  M_k − M_{k−1} = δ_k/k          x_k − M_k = δ_k − δ_k/k = δ_k (k−1)/k

Sum of squares (same idea: update the old quantity instead of recomputing)
  For i < k:   x_i − M_k = (x_i − M_{k−1}) − δ_k/k
  SS_k = Σ_{i

Code: the core is 3 statements. Using the already updated mean in the last one is exactly δ_k·(x_k − M_k).


let M = 0, S = 0, k = 0;
for (const x of data) {
  k++;
  const d = x - M;   // 1: δ_k
  M += d / k;        // 2: Knuth: M_k = M_{k-1} + δ_k / k
  S += d * (x - M);  // 3: SS_k = SS_{k-1} + δ_k (x_k - M_k)
}
// mean = M,  SS = S,  sample variance = S / (k - 1)

Discussion




Why Welford


The algorithm needs one pass and O(1) memory: a new score updates (k, M, SS) and can be discarded, which is what a stream of penetration attempts requires. It is also numerically stable. The textbook formula (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n−1) subtracts two huge, nearly equal numbers and loses all precision when the mean is large compared with the spread; the stability test above returns a wrong, even negative, variance for data around 10⁹, while Welford returns the exact value because it only manipulates deviations from the running mean.


Conclusions from the comparison


    

  • The average of the sample means matches μ for every n, even n = 2. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator: E[x̄] = μ. The small gaps in the table are Monte-Carlo noise (the z-score shows them in standard errors of the average over m hackers, and they are typically within about ±2).
    • 

  • The average of the sample variances matches σ² when the divisor is n−1: E[s²] = σ². With divisor n the average is smaller by the factor (n−1)/n (the third column of the table; for n = 2 it is half of σ²). This is the reason for Bessel's correction: deviations are measured from x̄, which is itself fitted to the same data and is closer to them than μ is, so SS is too small by exactly one degree of freedom.
    • 

  • Variability. Var(x̄) = σ²/n, so the spread of the sample means over the hackers shrinks like 1/√n. The variance of s² is μ4/n − σ⁴(n−3)/(n(n−1)) (μ4 = fourth central moment), also of order 1/n for large n, so both estimators concentrate around the true value as n grows (they are consistent, in line with the law of large numbers of HW4). The table compares the empirical and the exact theoretical standard deviations and the log–log plot shows slope −1/2 for both. Two differences: s² is noisier than x̄ in relative terms, and its noise depends on the tails of the distribution (fourth moment): the heavy-tailed truncated geometric gives a much more variable s² than the uniform or the binomial at the same n.
    • 

  • Role of m. m only reduces the Monte-Carlo noise of our averages (like 1/√m) so that bias or its absence can be seen; the variability of each single hacker's estimate is controlled by n. A real analyst has just one hacker, one sample: the histograms show how far x̄ and s² can be from the truth in that case. For large n the histogram of x̄ is close to N(μ, σ²/n) (CLT), while s² is skewed for small n.
    • 



Why this matters in inferential statistics


In practice μ and σ² are unknown and are estimated from a sample. Unbiasedness and consistency say that x̄ and s² are right on average and converge to the parameters as n grows; knowing Var(x̄) = σ²/n and estimating it with s²/n gives the standard error, which is the basis of confidence intervals and t-tests (μ is within x̄ ± t·s/√n); the dependence of Var(s²) on μ4 warns that variance estimates (and tests on variances) are fragile for heavy-tailed data. The simulation here is the empirical counterpart of these facts: the m hackers sample the sampling distribution of the estimators.
